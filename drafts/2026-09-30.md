### Idea 1 — Professional Insight: Intent Architecture: What It Actually Looks Like to Plan Content for AI Retrieval Instead of Keyword Ranking

**Pillar:** AI in Marketing / SEO & Content Strategy

**Angle:** The Week 108 Professional Insight (New Year's Eve) named intent architecture as the first of three 2028 content operations shifts: the move from keyword clustering to intent resolution as the primary content planning input. Week 109 delivers the practitioner framework — not a strategic observation but an operational tool.

Intent architecture is a content planning input method built around four questions that precede every content brief:

**Question 1: What is the underlying problem this person has?** Not the keyword they searched. The problem the keyword points to. "Content strategy AI tools" as a keyword points to several different underlying problems: a marketing manager who has a manual content process and wants to automate it; a CMO who wants to understand what AI tools their team should adopt; a content writer who is worried about their role and wants to know what AI tools they should learn. The keyword is the same. The problem — and the content that resolves it — is different. Intent architecture starts by mapping the problem behind the keyword.

**Question 2: At what stage of awareness is this person?** Unaware (they have the problem but haven't defined it as a problem yet), problem-aware (they know they have a problem but don't know solutions exist), solution-aware (they know solutions exist but are evaluating options), or product-aware (they are evaluating specific products or approaches). The same problem, at different awareness stages, requires content built for entirely different entry points. Keyword clustering produces content for the search term; intent architecture produces content for the awareness stage.

**Question 3: What does resolution look like for this specific problem at this awareness stage?** Not "what does the content teach?" but "what does the person know, decide, or do differently after encountering the content that constitutes a resolved state?" Resolution is the content's functional output, not its topic. A piece of content that teaches keyword research techniques but does not produce a resolved state for the problem-aware content manager who has never built a content calendar is not resolved. It is instructional. Instruction without resolution is not AI-retrievable authority — it is information at the awareness stage of the person who already knows they need keyword research.

**Question 4: What format native grammar does this problem's resolution require at this awareness stage?** The long-form explainer is the native grammar of the solution-aware evaluator who needs complete comparative information before a decision. The short-form hook-tension-resolution post is the native grammar of the unaware person who needs the problem named before they can seek the solution. The podcast interview's question-answer register is the native grammar of the problem-aware person who needs the problem articulated by someone who has solved it. Intent architecture maps the format to the awareness stage, not to the distribution channel preference.

**The practitioner tool:** An intent map — a two-axis grid where the vertical axis is awareness stage (unaware → problem-aware → solution-aware → product-aware) and the horizontal axis is problem type (the three or four underlying problems the content program serves). Each cell in the grid specifies the content's resolution output and format grammar for that specific audience segment. The content brief is a row in the intent map, not a keyword cluster.

**Hook:** "The keyword tells you what someone typed. The intent map tells you what problem they have, how aware they are that it's a problem, and what resolution actually looks like for them. Those are different inputs — and they produce different content. Only one of them is what AI retrieval systems use to decide which content resolves the query."

**Why now:** Wednesday January 7, 2028. The first operational Wednesday of the new year. The professional audience is back at their desks, the planning sessions are ending, and the AI marketing practitioner who read the Week 108 New Year's Eve post about the three 2028 shifts is now ready for the implementation framework. Intent architecture is the shift that most directly challenges the current content planning workflow — it is where most content programs will need to make the most structural change — and January 7 is the highest-readiness moment for a content planning framework post. Closing question: "When you write a content brief, which question do you start with — the keyword, or the problem behind the keyword?"

### Idea 3 — Wellness Tip: Peak Week Protocol: The Final Seven Days Before the Stage and the Three Decisions That Separate the Competitor Who Peaks on Time from the One Who Already Did

**Pillar:** Wellness & Discipline

**Angle:** The third preparation block (weeks 9–12, drafted January 2) introduced the cardio structure shift, the load expectation reset, and the timeline fixation risk. Competitors who executed the third block correctly and avoided the week-12 addition error are now entering the final phase: the peak week — the seven days before the competition stage where the preparation cycle's entire output is either displayed correctly or managed incorrectly.

Peak week is not a conditioning phase. The conditioning is already determined — the output of weeks 1–12 is in the physique. Peak week is a display management phase: the goal is to ensure that the conditioning already produced arrives at the stage at its fullest expression. Three decisions determine whether it does.

**Decision 1: Carbohydrate manipulation timing.** The peak week carbohydrate protocol is a depletion-load sequence: three days of reduced carbohydrate intake (completing the glycogen depletion the preparation cycle produced) followed by two to three days of elevated carbohydrate intake (the glycogen load that produces the muscle fullness visible on stage). The most common peak week error: loading too early. A competitor who begins the carbohydrate load four days before the competition rather than two to three days before will peak on the day before the competition, not on competition day. The glycogen load's visible effect (muscle fullness, skin tightness) peaks approximately 48–72 hours after the load begins. Load timing backward from competition day, not forward from the depletion end.

**Decision 2: Water and sodium management — what not to do.** The peak week water and sodium protocol is one of the most mismanaged elements of competition preparation because it is the element most driven by anecdote rather than physiology. The correct protocol is minimal intervention: sodium follows carbohydrates (as carbohydrate intake increases, sodium increases proportionally — no sodium restriction), and water follows training (increased training volume in the first half of peak week, decreased in the second half, produces the training-driven water output the physiology manages without external restriction). The error: voluntary water and sodium restriction three to four days before the competition, which produces a dehydrated, flat physique — the exact opposite of the muscle fullness the load phase is designed to produce. Restriction adds nothing to stage conditioning that the glycogen load protocol does not already produce. It removes the substrate that makes the load visible.

**Decision 3: The mirror test and the impulse to add.** Every competitor enters the final 48 hours of peak week looking at a physique that does not yet look like competition day. The glycogen load is still processing. The skin is not yet fully tight. The conditioning the preparation produced is not yet fully visible. This is the peak week's version of the third block's timeline fixation risk: the competitor who reads the 48-hour mirror assessment as a conditioning gap to close makes a last-minute addition — extra cardio, a sauna session, a restriction intervention — that interferes with the load the body is still processing. The conditioning that is arriving in 48 hours becomes the conditioning that will arrive in 72 hours, after the competition date. The correct orientation in the final 48 hours: execute the protocol. What is visible on competition morning is what the preparation cycle produced. The mirror 48 hours before the competition is not the competition assessment.

**Hook:** "Peak week is not when you get in shape. Peak week is when you display the conditioning you've already built — or interfere with it. Most competitors enter the final seven days and try to improve. The competitor who peaks on competition day executes the protocol and trusts the preparation."

**Why now:** Friday January 9, 2028. Competitors who followed the preparation arc (Weeks 104–108) and entered the third block on January 2 are, depending on their competition timeline, 5–8 weeks from their competition date. The peak week post is forward-looking: it gives the third-block competitor the framework before they need it, at the moment in the arc when they have enough preparation context to internalize the peak week logic (they understand the lag principle from the third block; peak week's load timing is the same lag logic applied to the final display phase). The post also reaches the broader fitness audience who has experienced peak-week interference — the sauna session that flattened the physique, the carbohydrate restriction that produced a dehydrated stage appearance — and gives the physiological framework retroactively.

---

January 1 is for declarations.

January 8 is for decisions.

And the gap between those two days reveals more about your year than anything you wrote in the first week.

---

I'm entering week 10 of competition preparation today.

The training session on January 8 is the same session it would have been without a new year beginning. The competition is the same number of days away it was on December 31. The off-season work I did in September and October is in the physique now — it doesn't reset with the calendar.

There is no resolution energy to convert.

Not because I'm exceptionally disciplined. Because I never needed a resolution. I made the plan in November, when the off-season ended and the preparation cycle started.

Week 10 is not a new beginning. It is week 10.

---

This is not a discipline story. It is a structural observation about what January 8 actually reveals.

The practitioner who enters the second week of January with clear direction and specific work to execute didn't find that clarity in week 1's planning sessions.

They built it in Q4.

The practitioner who enters week 2 feeling the gap between the declaration and the actual work didn't lose motivation. They deferred the development to January and are encountering something real: resolution energy doesn't convert to execution energy under the pressure of actual deliverables.

**Week 2's quality is the Q4 audit.**

---

Not every Q4 should be development. Some Q4s are delivery quarters — the year's biggest commitments closing out, the pipeline at full volume, no room to step back. That's real.

But if Q4 was a delivery quarter by choice, January's week 2 is where the cost of that choice shows up — not as a failure of motivation but as a structural gap between where the year needs to start and where the development cycle left off.

The gap is not closed with a better planning system in week 1. It's closed with the Q4 development work that wasn't done.

---

The question I find useful going into the second week of any year:

**What did Q4 produce that week 2 executes from?**

Not "what do I want to achieve in 2028?"

What did the Q4 development cycle produce — specifically — that January's work builds from?

If the answer is specific — a competency developed, an initiative 80% complete, a Q1 strategy designed and ready to execute — week 2 is a continuation.

If the answer is general — a fresh energy, a set of intentions, a clear picture of what you want the year to look like — week 2 is a conversion attempt.

Continuation is easier than conversion. By a significant margin.

---

I'm not dismissing the resolution tradition.

I'm describing what happens to it in week 2.

The year's arc doesn't form in the week when you declare what you want.

It forms in the week when the declaration meets the actual work — and either holds or reveals what the Q4 before it produced.

---

What does week 2 of January 2028 look like in your professional arc — continuation or conversion?
